Safety
Breast Implant Safety
Most people who have implants are satisﬁed, but implants have documented risks that do not disappear with
time. This page sets out what the evidence and regulators actually say.
Implant safety is not a single yes-or-no. It is a set of known, quantiﬁed risks — some common and manageable,
some rare and serious — against beneﬁts that many patients ﬁnd worthwhile. Reading them honestly is the
basis of informed consent.
The main risks
What can happen, and how common it is
Risk What it is What to understand Capsular contracture Tightening of the scar capsule around the implant The
most common reason for revision; can cause ﬁrmness, distortion and discomfort. Textured surfaces were
introduced partly to reduce it. Rupture / silent rupture Loss of implant integrity Silicone rupture may cause no
symptoms, which is why screening is recommended; saline rupture is usually obvious as the breast deﬂates.
BIA-ALCL Breast implant-associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma A rare lymphoma of the ﬂuid or capsule,
linked mainly to textured implants. It is treatable, particularly when caught early. BIA-SCC Breast implant-
associated squamous cell carcinoma A very rare cancer in the capsule; regulators list it alongside ALCL as a
device-associated risk. Infection Infection around the implant May require antibiotics and, in some cases,
implant removal. Systemic symptoms (“BII”) Self-reported fatigue, “brain fog”, joint pain and rash Widely
reported but not an established diagnosis; reviews ﬁnd the correlation with implants is not established and
evidence is limited. Sensation & breastfeeding changes Altered nipple or skin sensation; eﬀects on feeding
Varies with technique and individual anatomy; discussed before consent. Need for revision Further surgery at
some point Reported revision rates after primary augmentation are as high as 36%.
Monitoring, not reassurance
For silicone implants the FDA recommends MRI or ultrasound at ﬁve to six years after placement and then
every two to three years, because a ruptured implant can produce no symptoms. Ongoing monitoring is part of
living with implants — not a sign that something has gone wrong.
What the systematic reviews show
The Cochrane Library holds independent systematic reviews and registered protocols on breast implants,
wound drains, implant types and reconstruction. Systematic reviews pool many studies and sit at the top of the
evidence ladder, which makes them the most reliable single guide to what is known and, equally importantly, to
where the evidence is still missing.
The literature also documents past device failures and withdrawals — from PIP implants, with their increased
rupture and leakage risk, to the soybean-oil-ﬁlled Trilucent implants that were withdrawn because of lipid-
oxidation metabolites. These episodes are why modern regulators require long-term data and registries.

Breast Implants: Types, Placement and Long-
Term Reality
Breast implants are medical devices placed surgically to increase breast volume. They are the most studied
enlargement method — with real complication and revision rates that every patient should understand before
deciding.
Not the same as injectable silicone
Injectable silicone is a diﬀerent product from a silicone gel-ﬁlled implant and must never be used as a substitute.
Injectable silicone used to enlarge the breasts can cause serious, potentially permanent harm.
An implant is a device — a shell ﬁlled with saline (sterile salt water) or silicone gel — placed behind the breast
tissue or behind the pectoral muscle. Implant surgery is the best-documented route to a larger, predictable
breast size, but it is surgery: it carries risks, it is not permanent, and follow-up over the years is part of the
decision.
Implant types
Filling, surface and shape
Choice Options Trade-oﬀs to understand Filling Saline (salt water) or silicone gel Silicone gel tends to feel more
like natural breast tissue and does not collapse if it leaks; it requires monitoring for silent rupture. Saline
collapses and is absorbed if the shell leaks. Regulators approve saline from age 18 and silicone from age 22 in
the US. Surface Smooth or textured Smooth implants tend to feel softer and move naturally but may ripple;
textured implants are less likely to move but are where implant-associated ALCL occurs most often. Shape
Round or form-stable (“gummy bear”) Round gives a fuller look with less concern about rotation; shaped
implants give more controlled projection but can look unusual if they rotate and need a longer incision. Proﬁle
Low to high projection Proﬁle determines how far the implant projects from the chest wall; it must match the
width of your chest to look proportionate.
Placement and incisions
The implant can sit under the pectoral muscle (submuscular) or behind the breast tissue over the muscle
(subglandular). Access is usually through the inframammary fold (under the breast), around the areola , or in the
armpit (transaxillary). The route aﬀects scarring and, to some extent, how the implant behaves.
There is no single best combination. Nava and colleagues reviewed the evidence and found no high-level
evidence identifying one ideal technique or implant; complication reduction follows established surgical
principles rather than device fashion.
Longevity, monitoring and revision
Breast implants are not lifetime devices . There is no ﬁxed lifespan: some remain stable for many years, but

Decisions
Choosing a Surgeon
The single biggest determinant of a safe, well-judged result is the clinician you choose. Credentials can be
checked, and the questions that expose a weak consultation can be asked.
Advertising is not a credential. Anyone can describe themselves as a “cosmetic surgeon”, and clinics may use
the word “expert” freely. What can be veriﬁed is registration with a professional regulator, recognised surgical
training, and membership of a relevant professional body.
Veriﬁcation
How to check a surgeon before you commit
These steps are cheap, quick and entirely reasonable to insist on.
[Image: Stylised breast contour illustration with dramatic burgundy light]
Conﬁrm registration
Find the surgeon on your national medical council or regulator and conﬁrm the registration is current and
unrestricted.
Conﬁrm surgical training
A surgeon performing breast surgery should hold recognised training in plastic or reconstructive surgery, not
merely a short cosmetic course.
Check scope of practice
The clinician should operate within their qualiﬁcations; ask directly who will perform each part of the procedure.
Check the operating setting
Ask where the procedure is performed, who provides anaesthesia, and what arrangements exist for
emergencies.
Ask for the plan in writing
A reputable surgeon provides a written plan covering technique, risks, costs and revision policy — so you can
think it over.
Questions
What to ask at the consultation
Question What a good answer looks like How many of these procedures do you personally perform each year?
A speciﬁc number and a willingness to discuss outcomes, not evasion. What are your complication and revision
rates? Honest acknowledgment that complications occur, with ﬁgures or a range, not a claim of zero. What
would you do if I were unhappy with the result? A clear revision policy and costs, stated in advance. Which

options are you not oﬀering me, and why? A balanced comparison — including options that are less proﬁtable or
out of scope. How do you handle a problem after surgery? A named route for concerns, including out-of-hours

Red ﬂags that should end a consultation
•  Pressure to decide or pay a deposit the same day, or a “limited-time” discount.
•  Guaranteed results, guaranteed symmetry, or claims of “no risk”.
•  Vague or evasive answers about who performs the surgery and where.
•  Reluctance to provide a written plan, quote or revision policy.
•  Pushing an unapproved injectable, or refusing to name the exact product being oﬀered.
•  No discussion of alternatives, contraindications or what happens if things go wrong.
Why a cooling-oﬀ period matters
Cosmetic surgery decisions are permanent enough that reﬂection is part of good practice. Professional bodies
such as the UK’s BAPRAS describe a consultation and cooling-oﬀ period before consent precisely because a
rushed decision is a poor decision. If a clinic discourages taking time, that in itself is information.

Common questions
Breast enhancement FAQs
Can breast size be increased without surgery?
Some treatments are marketed for this purpose, but the evidence and regulatory status vary. Many non-surgical
approaches have not been shown to reliably increase breast volume. A professional assessment is the
appropriate way to establish what is realistic and which options, if any, are appropriate.
Are dermal ﬁllers safe for breast enlargement?
Dermal ﬁllers are not approved for breast enlargement, and regulators advise against using them for this
purpose because of potentially serious complications. A ﬁller approved for facial use should not be assumed
suitable for injection into the breasts.
Is PRP a proven breast enlargement treatment?
PRP’s eﬀects depend on the speciﬁc indication and the evidence available. It should not be marketed as a
proven method of increasing breast size without robust evidence supporting that particular use.
What is the diﬀerence between silicone implants and injectable silicone?
They are entirely diﬀerent. Injectable silicone is a diﬀerent product and should not be used as a substitute for a
breast implant. Injectable silicone used for breast enhancement can cause serious, potentially permanent harm.
Does fat transfer produce permanent results?
Some transferred fat can survive long term, but a proportion may be reabsorbed. The ﬁnal result varies between
individuals, and additional procedures may be considered.
How long does breast augmentation recovery take?
Recovery varies according to the procedure, surgical technique, individual healing and the clinician’s advice.
Your surgeon will give you a personalised return-to-activity timeline.
How do I know which procedure is right for me?
The appropriate option depends on your health, anatomy, expectations, risk tolerance and the evidence for the
intended treatment. The decision should follow an assessment by an appropriately qualiﬁed clinician.

How the procedure is usually performed
ASPS describes the operation in ﬁve broad stages.
Anesthesia
Intravenous sedation or general anaesthesia is used, so the procedure is comfortable throughout.
Incision
Peri-areolar, inframammary fold or axillary incisions are the usual routes; ASPS notes that the belly-button

Placement
The implant is placed either under the pectoral muscle (submuscular) or behind the breast tissue over the

Closure
Layered sutures close the tissue, with sutures, skin adhesive or surgical tape on the skin. Scars fade over time
and are aﬀected by genetics, nicotine and infection.
Results
Results are visible immediately, with the ﬁnal shape settling as swelling resolves.

Recovery and results (ASPS patient information)
•  Immediately after surgery the breasts are wrapped in gauze, with an elastic bandage or support bra to limit
swelling and support healing.
•  Patients are monitored in recovery and are usually permitted home about an hour after surgery once stable for
discharge.
•  The support garment (a bra or elastic band) is worn around the clock as instructed; wound care instructions
diﬀer between surgeons and should be followed exactly.
•  Acute pain typically subsides after one to ﬁve days, but soreness and swelling may persist for a few weeks.
•  Final results settle over a few weeks as swelling subsides; incision lines may take several months to a couple of
years to fade.
•  Breasts change over time with ageing, weight ﬂuctuation, hormones and gravity; some people later choose a lift
or an implant exchange.
•  Implants are not guaranteed to last a lifetime, so further surgery may eventually be needed.
Risks and safety (ASPS patient information)
ASPS lists the following possible risks of breast augmentation surgery, discussed fully before consent:
•  Anaesthesia risks.
•  Breast implant-associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma (BIA-ALCL) or other very rare cancers in the capsule,
such as breast implant-associated squamous cell carcinoma (BIA-SCC).
•  Bleeding or haematoma.
•  Changes in nipple or breast sensation.
•  Fluid accumulation (seroma).
•  Tight scar tissue forming around the implant (capsular contracture).
•  Implant leakage or rupture.
•  Infection and persistent pain.
•  Poor scarring, wrinkling of the skin over the implant, or the implant sitting in the wrong position.
•  The possibility that revision surgery will be needed.
For silicone implants, the FDA recommends screening with MRI or ultrasound ﬁve to six years after placement
and every two to three years thereafter. Risk factors include pregnancy, weight change and the menopause.
Routine examinations help conﬁrm that an implant is intact, because a ruptured implant sometimes causes no
symptoms.

Surgical · Corrective
Breast Filler Removal: Addressing Injected
Fillers and Silicone
Injectable ﬁllers and liquid silicone are not approved for breast enlargement. When they cause pain, migration,
lumps or infection, removal surgery may be required.
Fillers are not implants
Dermal ﬁllers approved for facial use must never be assumed suitable for the breasts. Injected silicone and
ﬁllers can migrate, form granulomas, cause chronic inﬂammation and infection, and complicate breast imaging.
They are not an equivalent or safer substitute for a breast implant.
People who have had ﬁllers or liquid silicone injected into the breasts may present with pain, hardness,
migrating lumps, asymmetry, skin changes or recurrent infection. Removal is corrective surgery: the injected
material is not neatly contained like an implant, and it interleaves with breast tissue, so complete removal is not
always possible in one operation.
Surgical approaches may include removal of the injected material together with the surrounding aﬀected tissue,
staged procedures, and in some cases combining removal with implant placement or a lift once healthy tissue
has healed. Imaging (such as ultrasound or mammography) is usually assessed before planning, because
injected material can distort images and mask or mimic lesions.
What patients should understand
Key considerations
Removal may be incomplete
Injected material spreads through tissue; complete clearance in one operation cannot be promised.
Imaging ﬁrst
Breast imaging is typically reviewed before surgery to map the material and assess the tissue.
Staged treatment
More than one operation may be needed, with healing periods in between.
Scarring & shape
Removal can leave contour changes; reconstruction options are discussed case by case.
Pathology
Removed tissue may be sent for histological examination, as routine.
Delayed presentation
Complications can appear years after injection, so history matters at consultation.

Questions
Breast ﬁller removal FAQs
Can all injected material be removed?
Not always. Injected silicone and ﬁllers intermingle with breast tissue rather than sitting in a pocket, so complete
removal in a single operation is not always achievable. The surgical aim is to remove as much aﬀected material
and tissue as safely possible.
Can implants be placed at the same time as removal?
Sometimes, but staged surgery is often safer when there is chronic inﬂammation or infection. Your surgeon will
advise based on the condition of the tissue.
Do injected ﬁllers aﬀect breast screening?
Injected material can distort imaging and make interpretation harder. It is important to tell radiologists and
surgeons about any prior injections, and to keep up with recommended screening.

Breast Lift (Mastopexy): Restoring Shape
A breast lift raises and reshapes sagging breasts by removing excess skin and repositioning the breast tissue
and nipple. It changes shape, not volume.
Sagging (ptosis) develops with ageing, pregnancy, breastfeeding, weight change and gravity. A breast lift
addresses it by removing loose skin, reshaping the remaining breast tissue and repositioning the nipple–areola
complex higher on the chest. Unlike augmentation, a lift on its own does not add volume — the breast is
reshaped using the tissue already present.
The degree of lift required determines the pattern of scars: around the areola alone for mild sagging, plus a
vertical line for moderate sagging, or an additional line within the inframammary fold for more signiﬁcant
sagging. Where more fullness is also desired, a lift can be combined with an implant or fat transfer, with the
trade-oﬀs of each discussed at consultation.

Surgical · Reduction
Breast Reduction Surgery: Relief, Proportion
and Recovery
Breast reduction removes skin, fat and glandular tissue to reduce breast size and weight, and repositions the
breast into a more proportionate shape.
Very large or heavy breasts can cause persistent neck, shoulder and back pain, shoulder-groove marks from
bra straps, skin irritation beneath the breasts, and diﬃculty with physical activity. Breast reduction addresses
both the symptoms and the proportions, by removing excess tissue and reshaping the remaining breast.
The operation is planned around your anatomy, the degree of reduction desired, blood supply to the nipple–
areola complex and your overall health. Scarring is an unavoidable part of the procedure; incisions are placed
so that scars fall within the bra line or along the areola border where possible. Because breast reduction is
performed to relieve documented physical symptoms, it may in some cases be approached through medical
insurance — criteria vary and require documentation.
What patients should understand
Key considerations
Symptom relief
Reduction commonly relieves back, neck and shoulder strain caused by breast weight.
Scars are permanent
Incision lines around the areola, vertically, or along the inframammary fold.
Symmetry is approximate
Perfectly equal breasts cannot be promised; some asymmetry is normal.
Recovery period
Several weeks away from heavy lifting and exercise, with gradual return to activity.
Sensation changes
Nipple and breast skin sensation may temporarily or permanently change.
Breastfeeding
Future breastfeeding ability can be aﬀected; this is discussed in advance.

Breast reduction FAQs
Can breast reduction be combined with a lift?
Breast reduction inherently includes lifting and reshaping the breast. A separate lift is generally not needed,
although the speciﬁc technique is tailored to each case.
Will the breasts grow back after reduction?
Reduced tissue does not regrow, but substantial weight gain, pregnancy and hormonal changes can alter breast
size again. Long-term results depend on stable weight and individual anatomy.
How much tissue is removed?
The amount depends on your anatomy, symptoms and goals, and is planned with your surgeon. The aim is a
proportionate, symptom-relieving result rather than a speciﬁc cup size, which cannot be guaranteed.

Surgical · Revision
Breast Revision Surgery: When Implants Need
Attention
Breast revision surgery is performed when a previous breast operation needs correction, adjustment or reversal
— for example implant exchange, removal, or repair of capsule-related changes.
Breast implants are not lifetime devices. Over time, the capsule of scar tissue that naturally forms around an
implant can tighten (capsular contracture), an implant can rupture or change position, or the breasts can change
with pregnancy, weight change and ageing. Some people also simply wish to change size. Revision surgery
addresses any of these situations.
Revision is often more complex than a ﬁrst operation: scar tissue, stretched skin, thinned breast tissue and
previous implant position all shape what can safely be achieved. Planning should therefore be led by a suitably
qualiﬁed surgeon who will examine you, review any previous operative records and imaging, and discuss
realistic options — which may include implant exchange, removal with or without replacement, capsulectomy, or
combining revision with a breast lift.
Common reasons
Why revision surgery is considered
Capsular contracture
Scar tissue tightening around an implant, causing ﬁrmness, distortion or discomfort.
Implant rupture or deﬂation
Loss of implant integrity, conﬁrmed by examination and imaging where indicated.
Position or shape changes
Displacement, rotation, bottoming out or an unnatural appearance over time.
Asymmetry
Diﬀerences between sides that developed after the original surgery.
Change of preference
A wish to change size, or to remove implants entirely (explantation).
Tissue changes
Sagging or skin laxity after pregnancy, weight change or ageing.
Revision is individualised
What is possible depends on the remaining breast tissue, skin quality, implant position and your goals. In some
cases a good result requires combining implant revision with a breast lift, or accepting that implant removal

Questions
Breast revision FAQs
How long do breast implants last?
There is no ﬁxed lifespan. Implants may remain stable for many years, but additional operations are common
over a lifetime of implant use. Regular review and prompt assessment of any new change are recommended.
Can implants be removed without replacement?
Yes — explantation without replacement is possible. The resulting shape depends on how much natural breast
tissue remains and the degree of skin laxity; some people choose to combine removal with a lift.
Is revision surgery more complex than ﬁrst-time surgery?
Often, yes. Existing scar tissue, altered anatomy and thinner breast tissue can make revision longer and less
predictable. A thorough consultation, including imaging where indicated, is essential before planning.

Surgical · Autologous fat transfer
Fat Transfer Breast Augmentation: What to
Know
Fat transfer breast augmentation removes fat from a donor area using liposuction, processes it, and transfers
selected fat to the breasts. It may suit certain patients seeking a relatively modest increase in volume without
implants.
The amount of fat that can be transferred safely, and the proportion that remains over time, vary. Some
transferred fat may not survive, and more than one procedure may be needed to approach a patient’s goals.
Fat transfer data from the American Society of Plastic Surgeons
ASPS describes fat transfer breast augmentation as using liposuction to remove fat from another part of the
body and inject it into the breasts, and notes that it is best suited to patients who want a relatively small increase
with a natural result. The ASPS average surgeon fee for breast augmentation with fat grafting is $5,719 (United
States, surgeon’s fee only). ASPS also refers to the technique as breast augmentation with fat grafting, and
cites research into mega-volume fat transfer with pre-expansion and into techniques intended to double breast
size using the patient’s own fat.

Surgical · Chest surgery
Mastectomy and Chest Contouring Surgery
Mastectomy removes breast tissue. When performed to create a ﬂatter, more contoured chest, it is often called
chest masculinisation or top surgery.
Mastectomy is the surgical removal of breast tissue. It is performed for several reasons, including gender-
aﬃrming chest masculinisation, reduction of elevated cancer risk in selected patients, and treatment of disease.
This page provides general educational information; the appropriate procedure, eligibility and timing are
determined through individual medical assessment.
When the goal is chest contouring rather than treatment of disease, the technique is tailored to the amount of
breast tissue, skin laxity and the desired chest shape. Approaches may include a double-incision technique with
nipple grafting, or a periareolar (keyhole) approach in suitable anatomy. The surgeon also considers scar
placement, chest-wall contour and future revision options.

Questions for your consultation

The approach depends on tissue volume, skin elasticity and the contour you want.

Where incisions fall and how nipple–areola position and sensation are planned.

Recovery

guaranteed.

Questions
Mastectomy & chest surgery FAQs
What is the diﬀerence between mastectomy and breast reduction?
Breast reduction removes a portion of tissue to make the breasts smaller while preserving a feminine breast
shape. Mastectomy for chest contouring removes breast tissue to create a ﬂatter, more masculine chest wall,
with a diﬀerent approach to nipple position and scars.
Will I need follow-up surgery?
Most people are satisﬁed with a single procedure, but reﬁnement or revision surgery is sometimes desired for
contour or scar reasons. This is discussed in advance.
How long is recovery?
Recovery varies with the technique and individual healing. Compression garments, limited arm activity and drain
care are commonly required for a period after surgery, with a gradual return to full activity as advised by your
surgeon.
