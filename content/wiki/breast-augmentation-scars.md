Scars are an expected part of breast augmentation surgery, and where they sit is governed mainly by the incision used to create the implant pocket. Their final appearance depends on incision choice, skin type, genetics, tension on the wound and aftercare. The sections below describe the usual incision options, how scars typically mature and which general measures support healing. Outcomes, risks and suitability vary between individuals, and an in-person medical assessment is required before any treatment decision.

## Incision options and where scars sit

Surgeons reach the breast and the implant pocket through a small number of standard routes. The choice balances surgical access, the implant type and [implant placement](/wiki/implant-placement/), the surgeon's training, and the patient's anatomy and preferences. Incisions are usually planned so that resulting lines fall within natural skin folds or along the border between skin and areolar pigment, where they are simpler to conceal in ordinary clothing. No incision leaves skin without a mark; planning aims to keep lines discreet rather than absent.

### Inframammary fold

The inframammary incision sits in the crease beneath the breast. It provides direct access to the pocket and is used for both silicone and saline devices, as well as for many revision procedures. The line is hidden within the fold when standing, although its visibility changes with posture and clothing. Where the fold is shallow, or where the two sides differ, the surgeon plans the crease position carefully so the scar does not sit too high or too low. Heavier or larger [breast implant sizes](/wiki/breast-implant-sizes/) can place more tension on this line during healing.

### Periareolar

A periareolar incision follows the lower border of the areola, where darker and lighter pigment meet. It can be a reasonable option where that border is well defined. The route passes through breast tissue, and surgeons discuss possible implications for nipple sensation and breastfeeding in general terms at consultation. Scar visibility depends on how the line heals along the pigment boundary, and this varies with skin type and individual tendency to pigmentation change.

### Transaxillary

A transaxillary incision is placed within the armpit crease, so no scar appears on the breast skin itself. It is used with particular implant types and operative approaches and depends on the surgeon's training and the patient's anatomy. Because the working distance to the breast is longer, later surgery is not always possible through the same route, and some revisions require an additional incision. Consultation therefore covers these limitations alongside the position of the scar.

### Comparing the three approaches

| Approach | Typical scar position | Points in favour | Points to discuss |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Inframammary fold | Crease beneath the breast | Direct access; commonly used for primary and revision surgery | Line may be visible in some positions; fold position needs planning |
| Periareolar | Lower border of the areola | Pigment boundary can soften the line | Passes through breast tissue; healing varies by skin type |
| Transaxillary | Armpit crease | No scar on the breast skin | Technique dependent; later access can be limited |

## How scars mature over time

Scar healing is usually described in overlapping phases rather than fixed stages. In the first weeks the line is commonly pink or red, slightly raised and firm to touch, and itching or tightness is frequent. Over the following months collagen remodels, and the scar typically becomes flatter, softer and paler. Maturation frequently continues for a year or longer, and some lines keep changing beyond that. These descriptions are indicative only; the pace and the final appearance vary widely between individuals.

Skin type and genetics influence the result. Darker skin tones may be more prone to pigmentation change, and some people form raised or keloid scarring whatever the incision used. The skin of the breast also carries tension, particularly with heavier devices, which can widen a line over many months. Terms such as hypertrophic and keloid scarring are explained in the site [glossary](/wiki/glossary/), which may help when reading clinical letters.

Patients often ask what they will see at each stage. In the early weeks the line may look prominent and feel tight; by the third month it is commonly flatter though still coloured; through the second half of the first year it usually continues to soften. Some people find the scar most noticeable at three to six months and quieter afterwards, while for others change is slow and steady. Clinical photographs taken at follow-up help track progress objectively once memory of the earlier appearance has faded.

## General scar care measures

Most surgical teams provide written aftercare instructions, and protocols differ between clinics and between patients. Measures commonly discussed include the following, introduced at the stage of healing your surgeon advises.

- Protect maturing scars from sunlight. Ultraviolet exposure can darken early scar tissue, so covering the area or using sun protection is commonly advised for several months.
- Consider silicone gel or sheeting once external healing is complete, following the clinic's instructions on duration and application.
- Keep incisions clean and dry as directed, and avoid soaking, swimming or heavy sweating until the team confirms it is appropriate.
- Avoid nicotine completely during healing, because it restricts blood supply to skin and is associated with wound problems.
- Wear recommended support garments and avoid strain on the incision during the early [recovery](/wiki/breast-augmentation-recovery/) period.
- Attend scheduled follow-up so that healing can be assessed and concerns raised early rather than late.

Massage of mature incision lines is advised by some surgical teams and not others, so it should begin only when wounds are confirmed closed and comfortable. Firm pressure over unhealed skin, early underwire bras and adhesive products used too soon can irritate a healing line. If dressings or tapes are used, instructions on when to change or remove them should be followed exactly, and any skin reaction should be reported.

## What can make scars less favourable

Several factors can work against neat healing, and many of them are modifiable before and after surgery.

- Nicotine use of any kind around the time of surgery and throughout healing.
- Wound tension from heavier implants, early heavy lifting or vigorous upper-body activity.
- Infection or wound breakdown, which makes widening or distortion of a scar more likely.
- A personal or family tendency to hypertrophic or keloid scarring.
- Poor nutrition, poorly controlled chronic conditions, or medicines that affect healing or bleeding.
- Sun exposure on immature scars, and delayed presentation of wound problems.

These points sit alongside the general [breast augmentation risks](/wiki/breast-augmentation-risks/) discussed during consent, and they are reviewed before surgery so that planning reflects individual circumstances.

## Scars after fat transfer

[Fat transfer breast augmentation](/wiki/fat-transfer-breast-augmentation/) avoids implant incisions on the breast in many cases, but small scars remain. Fat is harvested through liposuction access points, commonly on the abdomen, flanks or thighs, and these puncture marks mature like other scars. The marks are usually short, although their number and position depend on the areas treated. Donor areas can also change in contour, and access points may be visible on the skin of the breast where fat is injected. Sun protection, garment use and adherence to aftercare advice apply to donor sites as well as to the breast.

## Asymmetry, wide scars and revision scarring

Small differences in scar position, length or width between the two sides are common and often settle as healing progresses. Lines may widen when tension is high, and some patients notice a scar more in certain positions than under clothing. Scar position can also shift slightly as swelling settles and the implant settles into its pocket, so a concern raised at two weeks is assessed differently from one raised at twelve months. Where a scar matures unfavourably, options such as silicone therapy, injections, laser treatment or surgical scar revision can be discussed; these are individualised and are usually considered after maturation is complete. Further breast surgery may reuse an existing line or require a new one, as outlined in [breast implant revision](/wiki/breast-implant-revision/). No revision can be promised to produce a particular scar appearance.

## When to seek a review

Contact the surgical team promptly if a scar or wound shows spreading redness, increasing warmth or pain, discharge, bleeding, opening of the wound, fever, or a rapidly thickening or painful raised scar. Sudden change in breast shape, a firm painful breast, or a scar that concerns you also warrants review, as does any worry that arises between scheduled visits. Early assessment is preferable to waiting when something feels wrong, and follow-up forms part of the pathway described in [breast augmentation surgery](/wiki/breast-augmentation-surgery/) patient information.

## Scars at your consultation

Useful questions include where scars would sit for your anatomy, which incision suits the planned implant and pocket, how the fold or areolar border is planned, what aftercare is provided, how follow-up is arranged, and how scars are managed if healing is slow. Marketing language implying that surgery can leave skin without marks should be treated with scepticism; cosmetic advertising is regulated in India (NMC, ASCI), the UAE (DHA/MOHAP) and the UK (GMC, ASA). This page is educational and is not a substitute for personal medical advice. To discuss incision planning in person, you can [book a consultation](/in/book-consultation/) with the clinical team.
